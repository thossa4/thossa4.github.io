Mapping drought across Louisiana at 30 m

National drought maps cover large areas and miss local detail. This study combines Landsat 8 imagery, environmental data and ensemble machine learning to map drought vulnerability across all of Louisiana at field scale.

The problem

Drought maps that are too coarse to act on

Drought threatens ecosystems, farms, water supplies and communities. Louisiana felt this in 2023, when an extreme drought hit agricultural and hydrological systems across the state. The US Drought Monitor and NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information publish weekly and monthly maps, but they aggregate large areas and lack the spatial detail that local planning needs.

Approach

How it was built

  1. Satellite and environmental inputsLandsat 8 OLI imagery combined with environmental variables at a 30 m resolution.
  2. Ground-based training dataTraining data came from ground-based monitoring and government sources, split 80% for training and 20% for testing.
  3. Four models comparedRandom Forest, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), Artificial Neural Network (ANN) and Support Vector Machine were tested.
  4. Stacking the best performersThe best models were combined in different ensembles. XGB + ANN with a linear-regression meta-learner reached the highest test R² of 0.94.
  5. Regional cross-validation25 km spatial blocks tested how well the model holds up across regions. The XGB + ANN ensemble still performed best (R² = 0.85, RMSE = 9.57).

Findings

Where drought hits hardest

The southwestern, south-central and southeastern parts of Louisiana show high drought intensity. About 40% of the state is exposed to high or extreme drought conditions.

Why it matters

A low-cost drought monitor for local decisions

Beyond new drought insights for Louisiana, the study delivers a high-resolution, data-driven framework that state and local agencies, agriculture and water-resource managers can use for low-cost drought monitoring and management.
